# What it would take to feed the county

**A model of the nutrition, land, and labor to feed ~80% of Garland County — about 80,000 people — with honest numbers, ranges, and the one finding that changes the whole plan: in this county, land is not the wall. Growers are.**

Every building-block figure is cited below. The county totals are simple arithmetic on those figures, and the assumptions are stated in the open. Treat this as a planning model, not a promise — the ranges are wide on purpose.

## What one person needs a year

- **Calories:** ~2,000 kcal/day → **~730,000 kcal/year** (USDA Dietary Guidelines reference; real needs run 1,600–3,200/day by age and activity).
- **Vegetables:** the US eats **~414 lbs of vegetables and pulses per person a year** (USDA ERS) — MyPlate's target is about 2½ cups a day.
- **Protein:** 46 g/day (women) to 56 g/day (men) (National Academies RDA).

For **80,000 people**, the vegetables alone come to **~32 million pounds a year.**

## Three tiers — and what each would take

Feeding a county isn't one number; "fully fed" splits into very different asks.

### Tier A — everyone's vegetables & fruit *(the health-and-resilience tier)*
- **Land: ~500–1,000 acres.** Intensive market gardens can feed a lot from a little (one documented Québec farm feeds 200+ families from 1.5 acres); conventional production needs more. Plan on the middle.
- **Labor: ~300–600 full-time growers.** Diversified vegetable production runs roughly 850–2,000 labor-hours per acre (SARE) — which, divided into a 2,000-hour work year, is a few hundred livelihoods.
- **Versus today:** Garland grows vegetables on **27 acres.** This is a 20–40× increase — but on just **2–4% of the county's existing 29,001 farm acres.** The land is already here.

### Tier B — everyone's calories, from staples *(the survival tier)*
- **Land: ~3,800 acres if it were all potatoes** (potatoes yield ~15.3 million calories per acre — the land-efficient calorie champion), or **~8,000–15,000 acres** for a realistic staple mix (potatoes, sweet potatoes, beans, winter squash, grain).
- **Versus today:** that fits inside the county's 29,001 farm acres, though it exceeds the current 8,531 acres of cropland — a real land-use shift, but physically possible.

### Tier C — the whole current diet *(meat, dairy, grain, oil, everything)*
- **Land: ~80,000–200,000 acres** (peer-reviewed carrying-capacity research puts a full mixed diet at ~1–2.5 acres per person; the more meat, the more land).
- **Versus today:** Garland has **29,001 acres in farms.** Full local self-sufficiency for the whole diet would take **3 to 7 times the county's entire farm base.** It is not physically possible to grow the complete diet inside the county — and that's true almost everywhere. No county is a food island. You grow the resilient, health-critical slice locally and stay connected for the rest.

## The finding that changes the plan

Put the tiers next to what the county actually has, and the constraint flips from what everyone assumes:

- Garland County sits on **29,001 acres of farmland** and grows food-crops on almost none of it — **27 acres of vegetables**, with 73% of farm sales coming from poultry and livestock and only 9% of farms selling directly to people.
- Feeding **everyone's produce** takes a few hundred acres and a few hundred growers. **The land is there.** What's missing is the *growers*, the *training*, the *markets*, and the *cold chain*.

**So land was never the limit. Growers are.** That is exactly why the rest of this build exists — the hub, the apprenticeships, the hoop houses, the anchor buyers, the $400K of capital — none of it grows a tomato. All of it manufactures the missing ingredient: **people who can make a living growing food, and a market steady enough to keep them doing it.** Feed the county and you don't just get fed — you get 300–600 new livelihoods on land that's sitting idle.

## The honest caveats

- **Ranges are wide** because intensive and conventional production differ by 5–10×; the labor figures come from a real but non-random 19-farm study (SARE) and are directional, not a national average.
- **Diet drives everything.** The land numbers swing enormously with how much meat is in the diet — the whole-diet tier assumes the current American diet; a lower-meat diet cuts it sharply.
- **Staple yields** use USDA national/regional figures (sweet-potato yield is a Mississippi proxy); Arkansas-specific yields would refine Tier B.
- **This isn't a call to grow everything here.** It's a map of which slice is reachable — the produce and storage-staples that carry the health and resilience payoff — and which slice stays part of a wider system.
