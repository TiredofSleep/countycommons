# The two pots — who pays to feed a county, and why

**A funding map for treating food as a utility: businesses and people working together to keep everything cyclical — with the only dead end being the one we all have, food. And even that isn't really an end, because fed people are what grow the next round.**

Most community food efforts die because their money comes from *outside* and never circulates — a grant pays the coordinator, the grant ends, it stops. The fix isn't one clever funding source. It's understanding that there are **two kinds of money**, and a lasting food system needs both.

## The two pots

**The engine pot** earns its way. It pays for the infrastructure and the part that can be sold — the hub, the cooler, the van, the sales to anchor buyers, the produce prescriptions health care reimburses. The money circles: buy, sell, buy again. (The economics are laid out, with sources, at [the food flywheel](/engine).)

**The commons pot** is poured in as public service. It pays for the *"for everyone"* — the free food, the community fridge, the gleaned produce for people with no money, the garden anyone can use. It never pays back in dollars, and that isn't a failure. **It's not a dead end — it's the destination.** The dollars don't return because they turned into food, in people. Fire trucks don't turn a profit either; you don't measure them in revenue, you measure them in fires that didn't burn.

The two share one hub. The cooler that stores produce for the hospital contract also stores the free food for the pantry — so the engine's real job is to make every donated dollar feed *far more people*, because it rides on infrastructure the earned side already built. **A public-service commons at the core, wrapped in a business engine that stretches every donated dollar — the engine serving the commons, not the other way around.**

## Who pays — and why they'd actually do it

**The engine pot (they get value back):**

- **Oaklawn, hospitals, schools, the college, restaurants** — they buy food anyway; local wins on freshness, story, and procurement goals. It's a purchase, not a favor.
- **Medicaid & hospitals (produce prescriptions)** — feeding a patient produce is cheaper than the ER visit; they save money doing it, and Arkansas has the Medicaid waiver.
- **Restaurants & haulers (compost)** — tipping fees to drop food waste beat the landfill, and the finished compost sells. Waste pays twice.

**The commons pot (poured in on purpose):**

- **Churches / the food pantry** — feeding the hungry *is* the mission; no return expected or wanted.
- **Nonprofit hospitals (community benefit)** — legally required to spend on a named community health need, and food insecurity is named in their own assessment.
- **Foundations (Ross, Oaklawn, the community foundation)** — their charter *is* to give to things that don't pay back; food insecurity is a stated priority.
- **Government (county, city, USDA, CDBG)** — public welfare, and a fed, healthy population is cheaper and steadier than a hungry one.
- **Neighbors (mutual aid, tithing, the fund)** — they want their people fed; the return is belonging.

## Why anyone pours money into a dead end

1. **We already do exactly this.** Fire, roads, water, libraries, schools — none turn a profit; we fund them because a community with them beats one without. Food is more basic than any of them. That's the utility argument.
2. **It's cheaper than the hunger.** Hunger bills you later — ER visits, chronic disease, worse schools, lost work. Feeding people up front usually costs less than the downstream wreckage.
3. **Someone's already on the hook.** The hospital *must* spend it, the foundation *exists* to give it, the church is *called* to it. You're not begging — you're aiming committed money at its best target.
4. **It's a commons, not charity.** A fridge, a garden, a market — for everyone, like a public park. People fund a commons with pride and a handout grudgingly. Build the commons.

## What this platform does with it

A dead-end fund lives or dies on trust — people keep pouring in only if they can see the dollars turning into food. That is exactly what County Commons publishes: every dollar into the fund and every pound of food out, cited and dated, so *"pour money in as a public service"* is backed by receipts nobody has to take on faith. See the engine economics at [the food flywheel](/engine) and the county's own plan at [food as a utility](/food).
