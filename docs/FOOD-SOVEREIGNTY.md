# The path to food sovereignty

**Local food security that a community grows, owns, and takes pride in — not a market that feeds it. This is the goal the rest of the food work exists to serve, and a real path to it, from one garden to a county that can feed itself.**

Food sovereignty is *"the right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods, and their right to define their own food and agriculture systems"* — the food system serving the people who grow and eat, [La Via Campesina](https://viacampesina.org/en/what-is-food-sovereignty/) put it, "rather than the demands of markets and corporations." That's the frame. Not a business plan — a return to something communities did for most of history.

## The correction: full local food security isn't impossible — the industrial diet just made it look that way

An earlier version of this work called feeding the whole county from within "impossible" — 80,000 people needing 80,000–200,000 acres against a county that farms 29,001. That number came from assuming the *current American diet*, and that diet is the expensive one:

- In the diet closest to how America eats now, **only ~20% of cropland grows food for people — about 80% grows feed and forage for animals** (Peters et al., *Elementa* 2016 — the peer-reviewed carrying-capacity study).
- The same study found the baseline diet needs an **eight-fold** greater land footprint than the most land-efficient (plant-forward) diet — roughly **1.08 vs. 0.13 hectares per person** ([Peters et al. 2016](https://elementascience.org/articles/116); [Tufts summary](https://now.tufts.edu/2016/07/22/us-land-capacity-feeding-people-could-expand-dietary-changes)).

Run the county on a deliberate, plant-forward, low-waste sovereignty diet at that efficient end — about **0.3 acre per person** — and 80,000 people need **~25,000 acres: less than the 29,001 the county already farms.** Reclaim the diet and the land math flips from "3–7× the county" to "it fits." **Food sovereignty and dietary sovereignty are one project.**

And there's room to grow into: Garland County has **678 square miles — about 434,000 acres — and only ~6.7% of it is in farms at all**, with just **27 acres** growing vegetables ([USDA 2022 Census of Agriculture](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/AgCensus/2022/Online_Resources/County_Profiles/Arkansas/cp05051.pdf)). Land was never the wall.

## The local plate — reclaiming the diet, concretely

The diet lever isn't abstract; it's a plate, and protein is where it bites. Beef is the land hog — about **6 lb of grain per pound of beef** (or 7–25 lb of forage), versus **2.5 lb of feed per pound of chicken** ([Noble Research Institute](https://www.noble.org/regenerative-agriculture/livestock/the-efficiency-of-beef-production/)). So the sovereign plate keeps the efficient, local proteins as the base and makes beef the exception:

- **Eggs — the quiet MVP.** A good backyard hen lays **250–300 eggs a year** ([UNH Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/resource/producing-your-own-eggs)); a few hens cover a household's daily protein, and nobody controls them but you.
- **Chicken.** The most feed-efficient meat, and this is poultry country. The everyday bird.
- **Deer.** Arkansas hunters already take **~200,000 deer a year** ([AGFC](https://www.agfc.com/news/arkansas-deer-harvest-increases-just-short-of-200000/)) — ~45–60 lb of venison each, wild and near-zero-input, shared through the processor → nonprofit channel.
- **Beans & pulses.** The plant-protein backbone — land-efficient, storable, and about **1/13th the carbon per gram of protein of beef** ([Drewnowski & Conrad 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11377338/)); the [almanac](/almanac) already grows them.
- **Beef — the delicacy: a yearly ration for everyone.**

**The ration math:** Americans eat ~57 lb of beef a year each. Cut that to a **~12 lb yearly ration** and 80,000 people need ~960,000 lb — about **2,000 finished cattle a year** (a ~1,200-lb steer yields ~470–490 lb of freezer beef, [SDSU](https://extension.sdstate.edu/how-much-meat-can-you-expect-fed-steer) & [MSU Extension](https://extension.msstate.edu/publications/how-much-meat-expect-beef-animal-farm-direct-beef)). At Arkansas stocking rates (~2–5 acres per cow, UA Division of Agriculture), that herd is **on the order of the county's existing 10,820 acres of pasture** — the grass it already grazes, especially with rotational grazing. Everyday beef for 80,000 would take several times that. **Beef-as-ration is exactly what keeps local protein inside the county's own land.**

And the framing is the gift: a yearly ration turns scarcity into **ceremony** — a *community beef share*, animals raised on the commons and divided among every household each fall. Not "you can't have beef," but "beef is special, and everyone gets their share." A feast, and a commons (a community cattle co-op, everyone a member). *(Rough figures from sourced building blocks; the Arkansas stocking rate should be re-checked against the primary PDF before it's quoted as hard.)*

## The proof it's real, not utopian

This has been done — at national scale, in living memory, without the market:

- **In 1943, victory gardens grew 42% of all the produce in the United States** — from more than **20 million** household and community gardens, ordinary people growing ~10 billion pounds of food, driven by pride and necessity, not profit ([National WWII Museum, citing USDA](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/victory-gardens-world-war-ii)).
- When the Soviet trade collapse cut Cuba off from food imports in the 1990s, its cities fed themselves on neighborhood plots and organopónicos. Havana's urban-farming workforce grew from 9,000 to over 44,000, and its urban farms are widely reported (FAO and Cuban data, via economist Sinan Koont) to have supplied **somewhere between half and the large majority of Havana's fresh vegetables** ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organop%C3%B3nicos)). Born of real hardship — cite it as proof it's *possible*, not as a model anyone would choose.

Communities fed themselves before the global supply chain existed. The knowledge isn't lost; it's just out of practice.

## How it scales — like a garden, not a company

A company scales by pouring in capital and growing one big thing. A garden scales by **replication and connection** — every gardener teaches the next, every plot seeds another. You don't need the last stage's institutions to start the first. That's why it can begin from nothing.

**Stage 0 — One garden, one table.** A household grows some of its own food, gets good at it, shares the surplus. Near-zero cost. *One household harder to starve.*

**Stage 1 — The circle.** A handful of families grow and swap — a shared work-day, a seed library, a canning day, a gift table. Skill spreads by doing. The diet starts bending toward what the land grows well. *A few families ride out a bad month together.*

**Stage 2 — The commons plot.** Shared land opens — a church lot, a $1 parcel, a homestead's spare acres. A community garden, a shared cellar, a gleaning tie-in, and the first **paid steward** (from the commons fund, not from sales). A training pipeline. *A neighborhood's pantry is fed by growers, and can take a shock.*

**Stage 3 — The town web.** Households + community gardens + small co-op farms + a shared cellar and kitchen — **held by a cooperative or a community land trust, not a business.** A food council governs it; apprenticeships mint new growers. *Most of the town's produce and a real share of its calories come from within — and no one who wants food goes without.*

**Stage 4 — County sovereignty.** A land trust holds real acreage in common, out of the market forever; a broad base grows the plant-forward diet that fits the land; storage carries it through winter; the skill is universal, taught to every child. The market touches only the edges, for what genuinely can't be grown here. *The county feeds itself — reached not by one giant farm but by thousands of hands and a reclaimed diet.*

## Four threads that thicken the whole way up

| Thread | Stage 0 | → | Stage 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diet** | private taste | → | a food culture that fits the land |
| **Land** | your own yard | → | a land trust holding acreage in common |
| **Skill** | one gardener | → | taught to every child |
| **Ownership** | private | → | commons and cooperative |

The market shrinks as you climb: money seeds the first steward and buys durable capital (a cellar, tools, a hoop house) early, then ownership moves to the commons and the system runs on distributed hands and sharing. A **community land trust** (the Agrarian Trust model — nonprofit-held land on long leases to farmers) is how the land comes out of the speculative market permanently, so it can never be bought, flipped, or foreclosed away from the people who work it.

## Measure security, not revenue

The scoreboard changes. Not dollars — **food security for all.** The platform tracks, in public: the share of households with reliable food access, the share of food grown locally, the number of active growers, the acres held in common, and the plain count of *nobody went without this month.* Those are the receipts for sovereignty — and watching that number climb together is the pride.

## Start from nothing — this season

Plant one garden. Teach one person to grow. Start a seed library. Open one shared plot. Hold one canning day. **Start the count.** Then replicate and connect — the second gardener is the whole strategy.

## The honest part

- **The diet shift is the hardest thing here** — harder than any acre or dollar. It's cultural and generational; it can't be bought or granted, it has to be *wanted.*
- **Land access is a real fight** — most acreage is tied up in pasture, private hands, or forest.
- **This is measured in decades and pride, not quarters.** You raise kids into it.
- **Sovereignty is not autarky.** Central Arkansas won't grow coffee or citrus, and that's fine — aim for control and security, the ability to feed yourselves, not 100% of every item. Chasing total self-sufficiency is the trap; chasing sovereignty is the goal.

Your real job isn't to build the whole thing — it's to make it **wanted** (the pride), **possible** (the land, the skill, the tools), and **visible** (the count that shows it working). Do that, and it spreads on its own, the way gardens do.
