# The food flywheel — how a local food system pays for itself

**A design for a food system that sustains — one whose operating costs are covered by earned revenue and circulating value, not by the next grant — with the evidence that it actually works, and an honest account of where it strains.**

Most community food efforts start and die. They die because their fuel comes from *outside* the community — a grant pays the coordinator, volunteers do the work, charity is the whole model — and none of it circulates. When the grant ends, the paycheck ends. When the volunteer burns out, the garden goes to weeds. A charity *consumes* resources. A system *circulates and regenerates* them.

## The engine, in one line

> **Grants buy capital, once. Sales buy operations, forever.**

Money that keeps the lights on comes from **selling to buyers who already pay**; grants get spent only on one-time infrastructure that keeps working after the money is gone. Two more rules turn parts into a system: **every output is another part's input**, and **the people whose livelihood depends on it are the ones who own it.**

## The loops that make it feed itself

- **Money loop.** Growers supply a **food hub** (aggregation, cold storage, light processing, delivery); the hub sells local food to **anchor buyers** at market rate; the money flows back to pay growers and staff. The hub's seconds and surplus feed the pantries — *the selling arm funds the giving arm.*
- **Material loop.** Restaurant, resort, and institutional food waste becomes **compost** — cutting the growers' biggest input cost and adding a second revenue line.
- **People loop.** **Paid apprentices** (workforce funds, a local college) replace burn-out volunteers and become the next growers.
- **Ownership loop.** A growers' **co-op** or **community land trust** owns the hub and the land, so the system survives any one founder leaving.

## Does it actually sustain? What the evidence says

**Food hubs are businesses, and most break even.** In 2020, **91% of food hubs reported breaking even or better**, up from about two-thirds in 2017 and 2019 ([MSU 2021 National Food Hub Survey](https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/2021-national-food-hub-survey)). The best-run quarter turned a ~4% profit, and hubs above $1.5M in sales averaged a positive margin ([NSAC summary of the survey](https://sustainableagriculture.net/blog/food-hub-profitability-study/)). **The honest complication:** the newest data shows hubs are *more* grant-reliant even as sales grow — **over 50% rely on grant funding** to enable institutional purchasing, and they cite uncertain federal funding as a top worry ([MSU 2025 National Food Hub Survey](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/increased-local-food-sales-to-schools-and-the-value-of-grant-investment-findings-from-the-2025-national-food-hub-survey)). So the realistic goal isn't "zero grants" — it's **grants as catalyst and capital, not grants as payroll.**

**The anchor demand is enormous and already paying.** US institutional food service spends **over $130 billion a year** — 2,000 hospitals, 7,800 schools, 100 colleges are organized around redirecting it to local/sustainable food ([Health Care Without Harm, Anchors in Action](https://us.noharm.org/healthy-food/leveraging-power-procurement)). Schools alone bought **$789 million** in local food in 2013–14, a **105% jump** in two years ([USDA](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2016/03/15/farm-school-programs-invest-nearly-800-million-local-economies)); farm-to-school districts now average **~$114,000/year** on local food, about **20% of their purchases** ([USDA 2019 Farm to School Census](https://fns-prod.azureedge.us/sites/default/files/resource-files/2019-Farm-to-School-Census-Summary.pdf)).

**"Food is Medicine" turns health care into a paying buyer — and Arkansas is in.** Medicaid Section 1115 waivers increasingly cover **nutrition prescriptions and grocery provisions** (up to 3 meals/day for up to 6 months), and the states with recent approvals **include Arkansas** ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/section-1115-medicaid-waiver-watch-a-closer-look-at-recent-approvals-to-address-health-related-social-needs-hrsn/)); the number of "Food is Medicine" waivers has more than doubled since 2021 ([Health Affairs Scholar](https://academic.oup.com/healthaffairsscholar/article/2/2/qxae013/7595880)). It works: a peer-reviewed evaluation of **9 produce-prescription programs, 22 sites, 3,881 people** found participants ate about a serving more of fruits and vegetables a day, with lower blood pressure, BMI, and blood sugar, and less food insecurity ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37641928/); [Tufts summary](https://now.tufts.edu/2023/08/29/produce-prescription-programs-yield-positive-health-benefits-participants)). *(The Rockefeller Foundation [projects](https://www.rockefellerfoundation.org/news/new-rockefeller-foundation-research-finds-food-is-medicine-could-unlock-45-billion-for-states/) $45B in state economic activity if it scaled nationally — but that is a model, not a measured result.)*

**Compost pays twice.** Composting operations run on **tipping fees plus compost sales**, and the finished compost **reduces what growers spend on fertilizer and soil** ([US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/approaches-composting)) — a cost cut and a revenue line from the same waste stream.

**Co-op ownership lasts.** A rigorous academic review finds **worker co-operatives survive at least as well as conventional firms** — in France, identical death rates over two decades — and tend to be *larger*, not the small niche of the stereotype ([Pérotin, for Co-operatives UK](http://efesonline.org/LIBRARY/2016/worker_co-op_report.pdf)). A **community land trust** can hold farmland out of the speculative market permanently — the model the first US CLT, New Communities Inc., used for thousands of acres ([CLT case study](https://www.cltweb.org/resources/clt-case-studies/new-communities/)) — though that same history (a 1985 foreclosure after discriminatory lending) shows land tenure needs legal and political backing, not just a good structure.

**Value-added incubators earn their keep.** A shared commercial kitchen is itself a business: New Jersey's Hudson Kitchen reports its member founders generated **$3.2 million in revenue and 120 jobs in 2024 alone**, having supported 60+ businesses since opening ([Hudson Kitchen](https://www.thehudsonkitchen.com/ten-years)).

## The honest ceiling — read this before betting on it

1. **"Self-sustaining" is a direction, not a switch.** The 2025 data is clear: hubs still lean on grants. The win is moving grants from *payroll* (which dies) to *capital and catalyst* (which compounds) — not eliminating them.
2. **It needs a business operator, not only a mission.** Someone has to run the hub like the business it is — buyers, margins, delivery on time.
3. **Anchor contracts reward reliability.** A hospital or resort won't buy until you can deliver volume and consistency — the chicken-and-egg every hub crosses first, usually with a few committed early buyers.
4. **Produce prescriptions need a clinical partner and a payer.** The Medicaid pathway exists; a clinic and a reimbursement arrangement still have to be built.
5. **This is a 3–5 year build.** The flywheel is slow to start and hard to stop once it turns. Checklists light the fire; this is what keeps it burning.

## What this platform does with it

County Commons doesn't run the hub. Its job is three things: **put the question to the people who decide**, **track the loop in public** on [what came of it](/outcomes) — each step dated and cited — and keep a public **local-food-dollar score**: how many of the county's food dollars stay in the county, moved from *reconstructed* to *earned*, year over year.
