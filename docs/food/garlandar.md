# Food as a utility — growing it together

**A proposal for treating access to food the way Garland County already treats parks, the library, and water: as shared infrastructure the community keeps up together — and an honest account of how far that idea reaches.**

Most food help is emergency help: a pantry box when a household has already run short. That work is essential, and nothing here replaces it. But there is a second thing a community can do that is cheaper, quieter, and durable — help people *grow and share food together*. Garland County is unusual in that it doesn't have to start from scratch: the pieces already exist and mostly don't talk to each other. The question this brief supports is narrow and answerable: **should the county back connecting and scaling community food-growing as ongoing public infrastructure, not a one-off charity drive?**

The need is real and local. Feeding America's 2023 estimate puts Garland County food insecurity at about **20%** ([Aspire Arkansas](https://www.aspirearkansas.org/families/food-insecurity)); the CHI St. Vincent Hot Springs 2025 Community Health Needs Assessment cites the 2022 figure at **19.6%**, and the **child** rate at **26.2%** ([CHNA](https://www.commonspirit.org/content/dam/commonspiritorg/en/chisv/soar/marketing/docs/pdfs/community/2025-hot-springs-chna-report.pdf)). Arkansas is the worst-ranked state in the country for food insecurity. (The two county rates differ only by data year — 2022 vs. 2023 — of the same Feeding America dataset.)

## The idea, in one line

Connect the gardens, the farmers market, gleaning, and the schools Garland already has into one standing program — and scale it — coordinated through one table, funded mostly by grants and volunteers, not new taxes.

## It already runs as a public service elsewhere — with numbers

- **Seattle P-Patch** — 91 community gardens run by a city department since 1973; gardeners donated 40,000 lb of produce to meal programs in 2023 ([seattle.gov](https://www.seattle.gov/neighborhoods/p-patch-gardening)).
- **Brown County, Wisconsin** — 13 gardens, 386 plots run through its Extension office, the county, and the city since 1996 ([UW Extension](https://brown.extension.wisc.edu/community-gardens/)).
- **Newark's Adopt-a-Lot** ($1/year) and **Baltimore's** (free) — a simple revocable license lets a government open land for gardens without selling it ([Newark](https://www.newarknj.gov/194/Adopt-a-Lot)).
- **Arkansas Gleaning Project** — 12M+ lb gleaned, in all 75 counties, ready to route Garland surplus ([arhungeralliance.org](https://arhungeralliance.org/programs/ar-gleaning-project/)).

## The north star — and why it's honestly out of a county's scale

**Belo Horizonte, Brazil** runs a "food as a right" system reported at ~2% of its city budget, serving ~10,600 subsidized meals a day ([NYC Food Policy Center](https://www.nycfoodpolicy.org/popular-restaurants-belo-horizonte-urban-food-policy-snapshot/)). **Rosario, Argentina** turned vacant land into 75 hectares of gardens, 2,400+ families, and 2,500 tons/year — winning WRI's Prize for Cities in 2021 ([WRI](https://www.wri.org/news/release-prize-cities-awarded-rosario-argentina-improving-resilience-and-equity-through-urban)). These are the direction, not the destination. Cite them as proof the idea works, never as a promise Garland will match them.

## The county-scale playbook — connect first, then scale

1. **Network the gardens that exist** (the Welcome Center's "Common Ground," the child-care center's beds, the Master Gardeners' plots) into one visible program, and **open public land** for more via a $1 / revocable license — Parks & Rec already keeps pollinator and sculpture gardens, so the precedent is there.
2. **Hub it at the farmers market** — plug into the Arkansas Gleaning Project, add a produce-sharing point / community fridge, and confirm (or revive) the market's SNAP match.
3. **Grow it in the schools and at NPC** — extend the child-care center's model district-wide; feed produce into the Helping Hawks pantry.
4. **Make the CHNA group a standing food-access working group** — the table is already sitting; give it a mandate.
5. **Fund it with money already aimed here** — the Hot Springs Area Community Foundation (food insecurity is a named priority), the City's CDBG cycle, and USDA's Community Food Projects grant through a nonprofit like Project HOPE.

## The honest ceiling — read this before backing it

1. **A garden supplements a household; it does not guarantee anyone's food.** A California study estimates a plot yields roughly $435 of produce a season (Algert et al. 2014 — widely cited; primary abstract paywalled). Real, but a supplement, not a substitute.
2. **Food insecurity is mostly about income, not distance to a tomato.** Growing helps at the margin and builds community; it does not fix the wages and prices under the ~20% number.
3. **The health evidence is positive but thin.** The best systematic review (Hume et al. 2022, *BMC Public Health*) links community gardening to higher fruit-and-vegetable intake while stating plainly that "evidence quality overall was low" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9229094/)).
4. **Volunteers burn out; gardens need water, soil, and stewards.** The programs that last have a paid coordinator and a stable home.
5. **Community fridges are citizen-led, not a government program** — the Freedge network (150+ US fridges as of 2021, [Shelterforce](https://shelterforce.org/2021/02/24/community-fridges-provide-vital-and-visible-relief-in-the-war-on-hunger/)) is mutual aid, a neighbor-run complement.

## Will it last — or just start and die?

A fridge or a garden is easy to start and easy to lose. Whether it *sustains* — pays for its own operations instead of dying when the grant ends — is a system-design question, answered in **[the food flywheel](/engine)**: sell to buyers who already pay, spend grants on capital rather than payroll, and let the people who depend on it own it. In Garland that engine is unusually close — Oaklawn, the hospitals, and National Park College are anchor buyers already spending on food, and Arkansas's Medicaid nutrition waiver opens a produce-prescription payer.

## What this platform would do with it

County Commons does not grow food. Its role is three things: **put the question to the people who decide** (this open question and the priorities board), **track the loop in public** on [what came of it](/outcomes) — each step dated and cited — and **keep score honestly**, how much of this is real and running versus proposed, with the food-insecurity number beside it, year over year.
