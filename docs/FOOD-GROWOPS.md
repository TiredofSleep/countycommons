# Grow-ops — design our own, grant-fund them, deploy them

**Not buying a vendor's box (the trap — their power bill, their lock-in). Designing cheap, low-energy, open, community-ownable growing units, paying for them with grants, and seeding them out to communities that own and run them. The tool, not the landlord.** Designing and owning the unit flips the verdict on "high-tech growing" — the same reason a Freight Farms container fails sovereignty is the reason a unit *you* design and open-source passes it.

**Five units, one loop.** Food for people (greens + potatoes), feed for the chickens (fodder + insects), and fertility grown on-site instead of trucked in — each unit's output feeds the next. Below is every unit with its honest role, and — this is the part most "closed-loop" pitches skip — exactly where the loop *doesn't* close, because conservation of mass doesn't care how good the design is.

## The rule that decides everything: energy (and ownership)

Commercial vertical farms didn't fail because greens can't grow indoors — they failed on the **electric bill**. One estimate: growing indoors could take *"5, 10 or many more acres of solar panels to power each acre of vertical farming"* ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/food-and-farms/why-vertical-farming-just-doesnt-work)); AppHarvest went bankrupt in 2023 after a $3.7B valuation. So the design rule is absolute: **passive or local-solar, never grid-LED as the whole model.** Get that right and the unit works; get it wrong and it inherits the bankruptcy math even if you built it yourself.

## Unit 1 — the greens unit ("spinach")

Leafy greens are the *one* crop controlled growing is genuinely good at, so a purpose-built greens unit is the right call — done low-energy.

- **Core: the Kratky method** — passive, non-circulating hydroponics invented at the University of Hawaiʻi (B.A. Kratky, CTAHR). A container of nutrient water, a net pot; the plant is watered by capillary action and an air gap feeds the roots — **no pump, no aerator, no electricity.** Leafy greens (lettuce, and spinach-type greens) reach harvest in **~6–7 weeks** ([Kratky / ISHS](https://www.ishs.org/ishs-article/843_6)). This is the opposite energy profile of an LED farm.
- **Optional winter structure:** a **Deep Winter Greenhouse** — passive-solar, thermal mass stores the day's heat, only a small fan draws power — grows greens through a cold winter with near-zero fossil energy ([UMN Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/growing-systems/deep-winter-greenhouses)). (A full farm-scale DWG runs ~$18.5k–$58k; a household Kratky setup is materials-only — the two are different tiers of the same idea.)
- **Design goals:** built from off-the-shelf parts, repairable by anyone, owned locally; supplemental LED (if any) only for the deep-winter weeks and only on the community's own solar.

## Unit 2 — the potato op (honestly)

**Not a tower or a silo-grow — that's a myth (no yield bonus).** A potato "op" is two modules:

- **Production module** — raised beds or grow bags at honest in-ground yields (~2 lb per plant, [CSU Extension](https://extension.colostate.edu/topic-areas/yard-garden/potatoes-in-the-home-garden-7-617/) — re-verify). *Honest gap: no university source cleanly confirms grow-bag vs. in-ground yields, so treat bags as a way to grow where there's no ground, not as a yield multiplier — grow in soil where you can, and measure the bag numbers in the pilot.*
- **Storage module** — the real superpower (your silo instinct, pointed right). A simple root cellar: cured potatoes (7–10 days), then **38–40°F at 85–90% humidity keeps them up to 8 months** ([U Missouri MP562](https://extension.missouri.edu/publications/mp562); [UAF Extension](https://www.uaf.edu/ces/publications/database/food/vegetable-storage.php)). Buildable as a ~4×6-ft below-frost-line space in a basement or garage corner with a vent window — or a CoolBot cold room. Calories that last through winter, as a kit.

## Feeding the chickens without importing the feed

The hidden import in "just raise chickens" is the **feed** — sacks of grain trucked in are the same dependency as the grocery truck. Two units chip at it. Both come with a myth to bust first.

### Unit 3 — the fodder / microgreens unit (greens, *not* a feed multiplier)

Sprout barley or wheat into a green mat in ~6–8 days and feed it whole. Tempting to think 1 lb of seed becoming 6 lb of fodder means you *made* feed — **you didn't.** Sprouting **loses 7–47% of the grain's dry matter** to respiration ([NDSU Extension](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/feeding-value-sprouted-grains); [Feedipedia critical assessment](https://www.feedipedia.org/content/hydroponic-fodder-production-critical-assessment)) — the weight gain is water. NDSU: "there is a net decrease in available nutrients in sprouting the grain." Feedipedia puts hydroponic fodder at **3.4× more expensive per kg of dry matter** than just feeding the grain. So the honest role: a **fresh winter-greens / micronutrient supplement** (vitamins, foraging enrichment), grown low-energy with a bucket-and-tray setup — never a way to stretch the feed bill, and never a complete ration ([Alabama Extension](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/farming/backyard-small-poultry-flock-management-series-feeding-the-laying-hen/): greens "sparingly," birds still need a complete base feed).

### Unit 4 — the insect-protein unit (black soldier fly larvae — *not* crickets)

**The cricket instinct is right in shape, wrong in species.** The one controlled study ([PLOS ONE, "Crickets Are Not a Free Lunch"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0118785)) found crickets on grain feed only ~35% efficient — *no better than the chickens themselves* — with **>99% mortality on raw food scraps**, and AAFCO has **no US poultry authorization for crickets** at all. So the unit is **black soldier fly larvae (BSFL)**:

- **~41.5% protein** (peer-reviewed average, [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9502457/)), raised on food scraps. A laying-hen trial fed **12 g live larvae/hen/day (~10% of intake) with no harm** and slightly *better* feed conversion ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7070753/)). This is the unit that genuinely turns the community's **food waste into eggs.**
- **The legal catch, named not glossed:** AAFCO's approved BSFL definition covers poultry **only when larvae are raised on certified feed-grade substrate** — *not* the food scraps or manure that make the closed-loop story sing ([AAFCO IDC minutes 2021](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Ingredient_Definitions_2021_Annual.pdf)). Feeding your *own* backyard flock scrap-raised larvae isn't federally enforced; **selling** scrap-fed larvae or meal as feed is outside the legal definition. (State-level backyard enforcement: **UNCONFIRMED** — no source found either way.)
- **Energy:** passive in Arkansas's warm months (~April–Oct); needs indoor/greenhouse siting or supplemental heat to overwinter a colony — a real but small input.

## Growing fertility instead of importing it

### Unit 5 — the fertility loop (recirculate everything; grow your "supers")

You can't truck in fertilizer any more than feed. So recirculate, and grow fertility on-site:

- **Recirculate:** clippings, spent plants, and kitchen scraps → compost (~1-1-1 NPK, [UMass](https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/vegetable/fact-sheets/compost-use-soil-fertility)); worm castings are richer (**1.94% N vs. 0.80%** for plain compost, [NMSU](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_h/H164/)); and the **frass** left over from Unit 4 is itself a fertilizer (**N≈3.2%, P≈1.2%, K≈2.9%**, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11050032/)). *Honest note: composting doesn't **create** nutrients — it cycles what's already in the inputs.*
- **Chicken manure is valuable but has a hard food-safety rule:** compost it hot (**131–170°F, 3 days aerated or 15 days windrow with 5 turns**), or wait **90–120 days before harvest** for raw manure (USDA National Organic Program). Non-negotiable on food crops.
- **Your "supers" — comfrey, honestly:** comfrey leaf is genuinely potassium-rich (~5.3% K measured in the Cornell/Unadilla trial; [USU Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/comfrey-in-the-garden)) and real as chop-and-drop mulch and "comfrey tea" liquid feed. Use the **Bocking 14** cultivar — sterile, so it won't go invasive. **But bust the myth:** the "dynamic accumulator mines minerals from deep subsoil" claim is *not* established science — the one rigorous field trial ([Northeast SARE / Cornell / Unadilla](https://projects.sare.org/project-reports/fne20-967/)) found plant tissue tracks the soil it's already in, and comfrey's roots are actually shallow. Comfrey concentrates the potassium *already in your topsoil*; it doesn't conjure new minerals.
- **Nitrogen is the one input you *can* pull from thin air:** legume cover crops (clover, hairy vetch, field peas) fix **50–150 lb N/acre/year** from the atmosphere ([SARE](https://www.sare.org/publications/managing-cover-crops-profitably/legume-cover-crops/hairy-vetch/)). This is the strongest lever for killing synthetic-nitrogen imports entirely.

## The honest shape of the loop — where it *doesn't* close

Two hard limits, stated up front because the physics doesn't bend:

1. **Grain still gets imported.** A hen eats ~100–150 g/day, mostly carbohydrate *energy*. Fodder (Unit 3) and BSFL (Unit 4) cover the **greens and ~10–20% of the protein** — real, and they close the *waste* loop — but the bulk **grain calories still come from off-site.** These units offset the feed bill; they don't achieve chicken-feed self-sufficiency. (That points back to the grain question on [/sovereignty](/sovereignty).)
2. **Minerals leak out with every egg and tomato.** Nitrogen you can refill from air forever (legumes). But **phosphorus, potassium, and calcium leave permanently in exported food, and there is no atmospheric process that replaces them** ([Virginia Tech Extension](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/CSES/CSES-98/CSES-98.html)). Comfrey relocates minerals; it doesn't create them. So a food-*exporting* system can cut fertilizer imports by half or more — but never to literal zero, forever. Replace the slow drawdown as locally as possible: return all residues, wood ash for K, bone/shell meal for P and calcium. "Radically reduced imports," never "self-sufficient forever." That's math, not a management failure.

## The grant map — who pays for what

Grants pay the **capital** (build and deploy), never the forever-operations — which is why low-run-cost passive designs matter.

| Program | Pays for | Award | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USDA Community Food Projects** | Equipment for the project; capacity to become self-reliant | $25k–$400k | Nonprofit/gleaner applicant (not individuals); match required ([grants.gov](https://simpler.grants.gov/opportunity/f065b979-761f-4efe-b456-a3da5db0bd0b)) |
| **USDA Urban Ag & Innovative Production (UAIP)** | Explicitly funds hydroponic/aeroponic facilities — *most on-point for a greens unit* | $100k–$250k | No cost-share; frame passive units as innovative production |

| **SARE Farmer/Rancher** | On-farm research & demo — perfect to *design and test* a unit | $15k (solo) / $30k (team) | [SARE](https://northcentral.sare.org/grants/apply-for-a-grant/farmer-rancher-grant/) — the R&D-phase grant |

## The open-source deploy model

**Own the design, open the plans, and communities everywhere build their own.** FarmBot proves the mechanism: it publishes hardware plans under an open-source-hardware license — *"free to use, repair, change, and share"* ([FarmBot](https://farm.bot/pages/open-source)) — so nobody is locked to a vendor. (Its own kit runs ~$6k–$8k, though — the value is the **open plans and the right to build**, not their finished box; a from-scratch build off the plans is materials cost only.)

So the model is a **hub and spokes**: a nonprofit/co-op *designs and refines* the units and publishes the plans (the hub); grants fund building batches; communities *own and run* them (the spokes); and any community anywhere can build their own from the open plans, with no vendor and nothing to repossess. Distribution at scale is proven — nonprofit "Garden-in-a-Box" programs have shipped tens of thousands of kits — just point it at food and open the design.

## The pilot — prove it in Garland, then deploy

1. **Build the loop small in Garland** — a Kratky greens unit, a potato production-plus-root-cellar kit, a bucket-and-tray fodder rack, a BSFL bin, and a compost-plus-comfrey fertility bed with a legume cover crop — on a church lot, the Extension demo garden, or a school.
2. **Run a season and measure** the numbers still unconfirmed: potato yield (bags vs. beds), fodder dry-matter and cost, BSFL yield and overwinter energy, frass nutrient analysis, and how much of the chickens' ration the loop actually offset.
3. **Open-source the validated plans** — parts list, build steps, the real numbers.
4. **Grant-fund a batch and deploy** to communities that each name a steward to run it.

A SARE Farmer/Rancher grant is built for exactly steps 1–3; CFPCGP or UAIP funds step 4.

## Honest gaps & guardrails

- **The potato container-yield number isn't university-confirmed** — measure it in the pilot before promising it in a grant.
- **Fodder is a supplement, not a feed multiplier** — sprouting *loses* dry matter. Fund it as greens, never as a way to replace grain.
- **BSFL, not crickets** — and if you ever *sell* the larvae as feed, the scrap-fed version isn't AAFCO-legal (feed-grade substrate only). Own-flock use is a different, unenforced case.
- **The loop doesn't fully close** — grain calories still get imported for the chickens, and P/K/Ca leak out with exported food. Claim "radically reduced imports," never "self-sufficient forever."
- **Manure on food crops has a hard rule** — hot-compost it or wait 90–120 days (USDA NOP). Don't skip it.
- **Keep it passive or local-solar.** The moment it leans on the grid for light, it's the trap again.
- **Every deployed unit needs a local steward** — a unit with no one to run it dies like any garden. Deploy the person with the hardware.
- **Open plans aren't automatically cheap** — the savings come from building off the plans, not from any vendor's kit.
- **Re-verify before you apply** — the Arkansas EQIP rate and several USDA dollar figures need a fresh check against the current notice before they go in an application.
